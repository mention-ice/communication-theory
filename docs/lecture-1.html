Summary

  • A message \(w\in\{1,\ldots,M\}\) is sent as the codeword \(x^n(w)\); the rate is \(R = (\log_2 M)/n\), and \(P_e = P(\hat{\rv W}\ne\rv W)\) is the probability of a wrong message.
  • The best decoder is MAP, \(\hat w = \arg\max_w p(w)\,p(y^n\mid x^n(w))\). With a uniform message it is maximum likelihood (ML): \(\hat x^n = \arg\max_{x^n\in\Ccal} p(y^n\mid x^n)\). On a memoryless channel it needs, for each channel use, the likelihood function \(a\mapsto p(y_i\mid a)\) up to a factor.
  • Binary input: one number per bit, the log-likelihood ratio \(L = \ln\frac{p(y\mid 0)}{p(y\mid 1)}\). BSC: \(L = \pm\ln\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\). BEC: \(L\in\{+\infty, 0, -\infty\}\). In general: any \(L\).
  • ML \(= \arg\min_{x^n\in\Ccal}\sum_{i:\,x_i\ne\hat y_i}|L_i|\), where \(\hat y_i\) is the hard decision: each disagreement costs its reliability. Hamming distance on the BSC, consistency on the BEC, weighted Hamming distance in general (equivalently, the nearest point \(\pm 1\) to \(L^n\)).
  • Two codewords are confused with probability at most \(\beta^{d_H(x^n,\tilde x^n)}\), where \(\beta = \sum_y\sqrt{p(y\mid 0)\,p(y\mid 1)}\) is the Bhattacharyya parameter of the channel. So \(P_e\le (M-1)\beta^d\): the minimum distance \(d\) is the exponent. For sure, \(d-1\) erasures (BEC) and \(\lfloor (d-1)/2\rfloor\) errors (BSC) are corrected.

In the demo, a phone recovered a message from a noisy recording. The microphone does not hear bits, it hears numbers. What should the receiver do with them? This lecture answers the question in four steps: what a channel is, what the receiver must keep from what it observed, what the best possible decoder does with it, and how often this decoder is wrong. A code is designed for a channel, so the channel comes first. Codes come next lecture.

1.1 The setting: channel, code, decoder

Notation (as in the Information theory course). A sequence of length \(n\) is written \(x^n = (x_1,\ldots,x_n)\). Random variables are upright capitals (\(\rv X\), \(\rv Y\), \(\rv W\)) and their values are lowercase (\(x\), \(y\), \(w\)). A lowercase \(p\) is a probability mass function or a density, and a capital \(P(\cdot)\) is the probability of an event.

Conventions. In the whole course, \(0\cdot\infty = 0\); \(a/0=\infty\) and \(a/\infty = 0\) for \(a>0\); \(\ln 0=-\infty\), \(\ln\infty = \infty\), \(e^{-\infty}=0\), \(e^{\infty}=\infty\). Two infinite numbers are never subtracted.

A channel \(P_{\rv Y|\rv X}\) has an input alphabet \(\Xcal\), an output alphabet \(\Ycal\) and a transition law \(p(y\mid x)\): the probability of observing \(y\) when \(x\) is sent. It is a probability mass function for discrete \(y\) and a density for continuous \(y\), and we shall not need more than these two cases.1 Over \(n\) uses, the channel is memoryless if

\[p(y^n\mid x^n) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(y_i\mid x_i). \tag{1.1}\]

The transmitter wants to send a message \(w\in\{1,\ldots,M\}\). An encoder maps each message \(w\) to a codeword \(x^n(w)\in\Xcal^n\), and different messages have different codewords. The code of length \(n\) is the set of codewords, \(\Ccal = \{x^n(1),\ldots,x^n(M)\}\subseteq\Xcal^n\). Its rate is

\[R := \frac{\log_2 M}{n} \quad\text{bits per channel use}.\]

For a binary code that carries \(k\) information bits, the message is the bit sequence \(u^k\), so \(M = 2^k\) and \(R = k/n\).

A decoder is a map \(y^n\mapsto\hat w(y^n)\in\{1,\ldots,M\}\). The message \(\rv W\) is random, with probabilities \(p(w) = P(\rv W = w)\). The decoder makes an error when its estimate is not the message that was sent, and its error probability is

\[P_e := P\bigl(\hat{\rv W}\ne\rv W\bigr), \qquad \hat{\rv W} = \hat w(\rv Y^n).\]

This is the probability of a wrong message. Since the encoder is one-to-one, choosing a message is choosing a codeword, and we also write the decision as \(\hat x^n = x^n(\hat w)\). So \(P_e\) depends on the set \(\Ccal\) and on the decoder, not on which message is given which codeword. The fraction of wrong information bits is another quantity, and Lecture 2 compares the two.

Proposition (the best decoder). The error probability is minimised by the maximum a posteriori (MAP) decoder, which picks the most probable message given the observation:

\[\hat w(y^n) = \arg\max_{w\in\{1,\ldots,M\}} p(w\mid y^n) = \arg\max_{w}\, p(w)\,p\bigl(y^n\mid x^n(w)\bigr).\]

When the message is uniform, \(p(w) = 1/M\), this is the maximum-likelihood (ML) decoder

\[\hat w(y^n) = \arg\max_{w}\, p\bigl(y^n\mid x^n(w)\bigr), \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \hat x^n = \arg\max_{x^n\in\Ccal} p(y^n\mid x^n). \tag{1.2}\]

Here \(p(y^n\mid x^n)\), read as a function of the codeword \(x^n\) for the observed \(y^n\), is the likelihood of \(x^n\).

Proof.

By Bayes’ rule, the probability that the message is \(w\) when the observation is \(y^n\), called the posterior probability of \(w\), is

\[p(w\mid y^n) := P(\rv W = w\mid \rv Y^n = y^n) = \frac{p(w)\,p\bigl(y^n\mid x^n(w)\bigr)}{p(y^n)},\]

where \(p(y^n) = \sum_{w'} p(w')\,p(y^n\mid x^n(w'))\) is the law of the observation. Now take any decoder. When the observation is \(y^n\), it announces the message \(\hat w(y^n)\), and it is right if this is the message that was sent. So

\[1 - P_e = \sum_{y^n} p(y^n)\,P\bigl(\rv W = \hat w(y^n)\mid \rv Y^n = y^n\bigr) \le \sum_{y^n} p(y^n)\max_{w} p(w\mid y^n),\]

with equality for the MAP decoder (the sums over \(y^n\) are integrals for continuous outputs). The denominator \(p(y^n)\) does not depend on \(w\), which gives the second form of the MAP rule. If \(p(w) = 1/M\), the factor \(p(w)\) does not depend on \(w\) either, and only the likelihood remains.

If several messages reach the maximum, any of them can be chosen. From now on, and in the rest of the course, the message is uniform and the best decoder is ML. For a memoryless channel, taking the logarithm of (1.1) turns the product into a sum:

\[\hat x^n = \arg\max_{x^n\in\Ccal} \sum_{i=1}^n \ln p(y_i\mid x_i). \tag{1.3}\]

What the receiver should keep. In (1.3), the observation \(y_i\) enters only through the likelihood function \(a\mapsto p(y_i\mid a)\), \(a\in\Xcal\). Multiplying this function by a positive factor that depends only on \(y_i\) shifts the scores of all codewords by the same amount, so the decoder needs it only up to such a factor.

So a good receiver hands the decoder, for each channel use, \(|\Xcal|\) numbers defined up to a common factor. From now until Lecture 3 the input is binary. Larger alphabets come back with Reed–Solomon codes in Lecture 4.

1.2 Binary input: LLRs and ML decoding

Let the input be a bit \(x\in\{0,1\}\). The likelihood function has two values, \(p(y\mid 0)\) and \(p(y\mid 1)\). Up to a factor, it is one number, their ratio.

Definition. The log-likelihood ratio (LLR) of an observation \(y\) is

\[L(y) := \ln\frac{p(y\mid 0)}{p(y\mid 1)} \ \in [-\infty,+\infty]. \tag{1.4}\]

One of the two likelihoods can be zero. Then \(L=+\infty\) or \(L=-\infty\), by the conventions of Section 1.1. They cannot both be zero, because such a \(y\) is never observed.

The sign of \(L\) says which bit is more likely, \(L>0\) for \(0\) and \(L<0\) for \(1\), and \(|L|\) says how sure we are. If the bit \(\rv X\) is uniform, Bayes’ rule gives

\[P(\rv X=0\mid \rv Y=y) = \frac{p(y\mid 0)}{p(y\mid 0)+p(y\mid 1)} = \frac{1}{1+e^{-L(y)}}.\]

For instance, \(L = 0\) means “no idea” (\(1/2\)), \(L=1\) gives \(0.73\), \(L=3\) gives \(0.95\) and \(L=+\infty\) means “\(0\) for sure”.

Let \(\hat y\) be the more likely bit, called the hard decision: \(\hat y=0\) if \(L>0\) and \(\hat y=1\) if \(L<0\) (if \(L=0\), either value will do). Its likelihood \(p_{\rv Y|\rv X}(y\mid\hat y)\) is positive, and the likelihood of the other bit is \(e^{-|L|}\) times as large. The subscript recalls that this is the channel law \(p(y\mid x)\) taken at \(x=\hat y\), not a law of \(y\) given the decision. In one formula,

\[p(y\mid x) = p_{\rv Y|\rv X}(y\mid \hat y)\; e^{-|L(y)|\,\ind{x\ne\hat y}}, \qquad x\in\{0,1\}, \tag{1.5}\]

where the exponent is \(0\) if \(x=\hat y\), even if \(|L|=\infty\). The first factor is the same for both values of the bit. Everything that distinguishes \(0\) from \(1\) is in the LLR: its sign names the more likely bit, and \(|L|\) is the price of contradicting it. The formula also holds when \(|L|=\infty\): the other bit is then impossible.

Now take a binary code, that is, a set \(\Ccal\subseteq\{0,1\}^n\). A codeword is \(x^n = (x_1,\ldots,x_n)\), the LLR of the \(i\)-th observation is \(L_i = L(y_i)\), and \(\hat y_i\) is the hard decision on bit \(i\).

The Hamming distance between two sequences counts the positions where they differ,

\[d_H(a^n,b^n) := \sum_{i=1}^n \ind{a_i\ne b_i},\]

where \(\ind{A}\) is \(1\) if \(A\) holds and \(0\) otherwise.

Proposition (ML with LLRs). For a binary code on a memoryless channel,

\[\hat x^n = \arg\min_{x^n\in\Ccal} \sum_{i=1}^n |L_i|\,\ind{x_i\ne\hat y_i} , \tag{1.6}\]

a weighted Hamming distance between \(x^n\) and \(\hat y^n\). A position with \(x_i=\hat y_i\) counts \(0\) even if \(|L_i|=\infty\): the sum is \(\sum_{i:\,x_i\ne\hat y_i}|L_i|\).

If all the LLRs are finite, this is also

\[\hat x^n = \arg\max_{x^n\in\Ccal}\ \sum_{i=1}^n (-1)^{x_i} L_i . \tag{1.7}\]
Proof.

By (1.1), with \(q_i := p_{\rv Y|\rv X}(y_i\mid\hat y_i)>0\),

\[p(y^n\mid x^n) = \prod_{i=1}^n q_i\,\frac{p(y_i\mid x_i)}{q_i} = \prod_{i=1}^n q_i\ \times\ \exp\Bigl(-\sum_{i=1}^n |L_i|\,\ind{x_i\ne\hat y_i}\Bigr),\]

because by (1.5) each ratio is \(e^{-|L_i|\,\ind{x_i\ne\hat y_i}}\). The first product is positive and does not depend on \(x^n\), so the likelihood is largest where the sum is smallest. This sum has non-negative terms, so it is well defined even with infinite LLRs: it is \(+\infty\) exactly when the codeword is impossible. If all the LLRs are finite, \((-1)^{x_i}L_i\) is \(+|L_i|\) if \(x_i=\hat y_i\) and \(-|L_i|\) if \(x_i\ne\hat y_i\), so

\[\sum_{i=1}^n (-1)^{x_i}L_i = \sum_{i=1}^n |L_i| \;-\; 2\!\!\sum_{i:\,x_i\ne\hat y_i}\!\! |L_i| ,\]

and the first sum does not depend on \(x^n\).

The cost in (1.6) is the Hamming distance between the codeword and the hard decisions, with a weight on each term, the reliability of the bit: contradicting a confident bit is expensive, contradicting a doubtful bit is cheap. The rule holds for every binary-input memoryless channel.

The LLR as the output. The rule (1.6) uses the observations only through their LLRs. This is no accident: by (1.5), the likelihood \(p(y\mid x)\) is a factor that does not depend on \(x\), times a factor that depends on \(y\) only through \(L(y)\). So the decoder loses nothing if the receiver passes on \(L(y)\) instead of \(y\): two observations with the same LLR are the same observation for the decoder. A function of the observation that keeps everything the observation says about the input is called a sufficient statistic, and \(L(y)\) is one. The map \(y\mapsto L(y)\) does not have to be invertible for this, because what it forgets is of no use to the decoder. We may therefore say that the channel delivers the LLR itself, \(y_i = L_i\), and this description needs no assumption on the noise. In Lectures 7 and 8 we shall see how a real receiver computes the LLRs. For example, if the bit is sent as the level \(\pm\sqrt{E_s}\), that is, \(y = (-1)^x\sqrt{E_s}+z\) with Gaussian noise \(z\sim\mathcal N(0,N_0/2)\), then \(L(y) = 4\sqrt{E_s}\,y/N_0\): the LLR is the observation itself, on another scale.

Remark 1.1 (the picture with \(\pm1\)). Suppose that all the LLRs are finite, and send each codeword to the point \(s^n\in\{+1,-1\}^n\) with \(s_i = (-1)^{x_i}\). The sum in (1.7) is the correlation \(\sum_i s_iL_i\) between this point and the LLRs. Since \(\|s^n\|^2=n\) for every codeword, for any \(\alpha>0\)

\[\|L^n - \alpha s^n\|^2 = \|L^n\|^2 - 2\alpha\sum_{i=1}^n s_iL_i + \alpha^2 n,\]

so ML also picks the point nearest to the sequence of LLRs \(L^n = (L_1,\ldots,L_n)\), up to scale: minimum Euclidean distance decoding. This picture comes back in Lectures 7 and 8, where \(\pm1\) are the transmitted levels.

1.3 Three binary-input channels

Three channels come back throughout the course. The first one is the link as the receiver first sees it, and the other two are what remains of it when the receiver keeps less. Each one has its own kind of LLR, and on each one the rule (1.6) takes a simple form.

Three channels, three kinds of LLR.

  • General channel: \(y\in\Ycal\), with a law \(p(y\mid 0)\) when \(0\) is sent and \(p(y\mid 1)\) when \(1\) is sent. The set \(\Ycal\) can be anything; in the drawing \(y\) is a real number and the laws are densities. \(L(y)\) can take many values: each bit comes with its own confidence. This is the channel as the receiver first sees it.
  • Binary symmetric channel BSC\((\delta)\): \(y\in\{0,1\}\), and each bit is flipped with probability \(\delta\). Then \(L(0) = +\ell_\delta\) and \(L(1) = -\ell_\delta\) with \(\ell_\delta := \ln\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\), for example \(\ell_\delta = \ln 9\approx 2.2\) for \(\delta=0.1\). Every bit comes with the same confidence.
  • Binary erasure channel BEC\((\varepsilon)\): \(y\in\{0,1,?\}\), and each bit is erased with probability \(\varepsilon\), otherwise it is received correctly. Then \(L(0)=+\infty\), \(L(1)=-\infty\) and \(L(?) = \ln\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon} = 0\). A bit is either known or completely unknown.

Quantising the LLR. A receiver can also pass on less than \(L\): it quantises the LLR, and the decoder then sees another channel.

With two levels, the receiver keeps only the sign of \(L\), that is, the hard decision \(\hat y\). Some decisions are wrong, and the decoder sees flipped bits. If the channel treats \(0\) and \(1\) alike, as in the Gaussian example, both are flipped with the same probability \(\delta = P(L(\rv Y)<0\mid \rv X=0)\), and the decoder sees a BSC\((\delta)\). With three levels, the receiver refuses to decide when \(|L|\) is below a threshold \(T\) and declares an erasure. A few of the bits that it keeps can still be wrong. The BEC is the idealised version of this receiver, in which the bits that are kept are all correct. Three levels are not always better than two. The erasure forgets the sign of the doubtful bits, the hard decision forgets which bits are sure, and neither output can be computed from the other. With four levels, the sign of \(L\) and whether \(|L|>T\), the receiver keeps both.

Let us now read the rule (1.6) on each of the three channels.

General channel. Every bit has its own weight, and nothing simplifies: ML is minimum weighted Hamming distance decoding. A decoder that uses the weights is called a soft decoder. Two errors on doubtful bits can cost less than one error on a confident bit, which a decoder that is given only hard decisions cannot see.

BSC. If \(\delta<\tfrac12\), the hard decisions are the received bits, \(\hat y_i=y_i\), and all weights are equal, \(|L_i| = \ell_\delta\). The cost is \(\ell_\delta\,d_H(y^n,x^n)\), and ML is minimum Hamming distance decoding: pick the codeword nearest to \(y^n\). If \(\delta>\tfrac12\), then \(\ell_\delta<0\), the hard decisions are the flipped bits, and ML picks the codeword farthest from \(y^n\). This is not a paradox: flip all received bits and the channel is a BSC\((1-\delta)\).

BEC. An unerased bit has an infinite weight and an erased bit has weight \(0\). A codeword that disagrees with an unerased bit has cost \(+\infty\): it is impossible. All codewords that agree with every unerased bit have the same cost, \(0\). So ML is: keep the codewords that are consistent with what was received. If exactly one remains, it is the right one. If several remain, the decoder can only guess among them.

Example 1.2 (what we observe is never binary). Flash memory. A cell stores a bit as an electric charge, and the charge sets the voltage at which the cell starts to conduct. This voltage is a real number, and it moves with time and wear. A read compares it with one reference voltage and returns a bit: a hard decision, fast, and good enough while the memory is new. When the decoder fails, the controller reads the cell again with other reference voltages. It then knows in which interval the voltage lies, and this gives an LLR with several levels. A hard disk is similar: the read head delivers a voltage, and the bits come after a decision.

QR code. The camera gives a grey level for each small square. The reader compares it with a threshold and keeps “black” or “white”: again a hard decision. A square under a stain or a logo is unreliable, and a reader that knows where it is can declare an erasure (TP2).

In both cases the physical observation is continuous. The binary output is a choice of the receiver, made for convenience: simpler circuits, less to store, a simpler decoder. Example 1.5 shows what this choice can cost.

Remark 1.3 (the channel that outputs LLRs). Take any binary-input channel and let the receiver output \(\ell = L(y)\) instead of \(y\). The new output \(\rv L = L(\rv Y)\) is its own LLR: for a discrete output and a finite \(\ell\),

\[P(\rv L=\ell\mid \rv X=0) = \sum_{y:\,L(y)=\ell} p(y\mid 0) = \sum_{y:\,L(y)=\ell} e^{\ell}\,p(y\mid 1) = e^{\ell}\,P(\rv L=\ell\mid \rv X=1),\]

and \(\rv L=+\infty\) can occur only when \(0\) is sent, \(\rv L=-\infty\) only when \(1\) is sent. So “the channel outputs LLRs” is a consistent definition, and it covers every binary-input memoryless channel. The BSC and the BEC are the special cases in which \(L\) takes two values, \(\pm\ell_\delta\), and three values, \(\pm\infty\) and \(0\).

Example 1.4 (the repetition code). The \((n,1)\) repetition code has two codewords, \(00\cdots0\) and \(11\cdots1\), and rate \(R = 1/n\). In (1.6) the cost of \(00\cdots0\) is the sum of the \(|L_i|\) over the negative LLRs, and the cost of \(11\cdots1\) is the sum over the positive ones. So ML is a majority vote on the BSC. On the BEC it copies any unerased bit, and it guesses only if all \(n\) bits are erased, which has probability \(\varepsilon^n\). On a general channel with finite LLRs it adds the LLRs and looks at the sign.

For \(n=3\) on the BSC, the decoder fails when \(2\) or \(3\) bits are flipped: \(P_e = 3\delta^2(1-\delta)+\delta^3\). With \(\delta=0.1\), this is \(0.028\) instead of \(0.1\), and the price is a rate of \(1/3\). Longer repetition codes drive \(P_e\) to zero, but the rate \(1/n\) goes to zero as well. This trade is not unavoidable, and this is the promise of the introduction. The BSC\((0.1)\) has capacity \(C\approx 0.53\) bit per channel use: there are codes of rate \(1/2\), much longer and much smarter than repetition, whose error probability is as small as we wish. We take this as a fact until Lecture 11. The next lectures build such codes and their decoders.

Example 1.5 (one reception, three receivers). Consider the code

\[\Ccal = \{00000,\ 01011,\ 10101,\ 11110\},\]

with \(n=5\) and \(M=4\) (two information bits), so \(R = 2/5\). The codeword \(00000\) is sent and the receiver computes the LLRs

\[L^n = (+4,\ -1,\ +3,\ -1,\ +6).\]

Two LLRs have the wrong sign, but both are small. Three receivers look at them. The first passes \(L^n\) on. The second makes hard decisions and passes \(\hat y^n = 01010\) on. The third erases the bits with \(|L_i|\le 1\) and passes \(0\,?\,0\,?\,0\) on.

codeword \(x^n\)soft: \(\sum_{i:\,x_i\ne\hat y_i}|L_i|\)hard: \(d_H(\hat y^n,x^n)\)erasures: consistent?
\(00000\)\(\mathbf{2}\)\(2\)yes
\(01011\)\(6\)\(\mathbf{1}\)no
\(10101\)\(15\)\(5\)no
\(11110\)\(7\)\(2\)no

The soft decoder finds \(00000\), and so does the decoder with erasures. The hard-decision decoder sees \(\hat y^n = 01010\), which is nearer to \(01011\): it picks the wrong codeword. For the soft decoder, \(00000\) contradicts two doubtful bits (cost \(1+1\)), while \(01011\) contradicts one confident bit (cost \(6\)). Each decoder is the best one for what it was given. What differs is what the receiver kept.

1.4 Error probability and the minimum distance

We now know what the best decoder does with any code. How often is it wrong, and what in the code decides it? In Example 1.5, two wrong hard decisions were enough to fool the hard-decision decoder, while two erasures did no harm. To see why, start with two codewords.

Two codewords. Let \(x^n\) be sent and let \(\tilde x^n\) be another codeword. ML can prefer \(\tilde x^n\) only if it is at least as likely as \(x^n\). The probability of this event is the pairwise error probability

\[P(x^n\to\tilde x^n) := P\bigl(p(\rv Y^n\mid\tilde x^n)\ge p(\rv Y^n\mid x^n)\bigm| x^n \text{ is sent}\bigr).\]

Only the \(d_H(x^n,\tilde x^n)\) positions where the two codewords differ matter, because the other positions give the same factor to both likelihoods. On the BEC, \(\tilde x^n\) remains possible only if all these positions are erased, so \(P(x^n\to\tilde x^n) = \varepsilon^{d_H(x^n,\tilde x^n)}\). On the BSC, \(\tilde x^n\) is at least as near to \(y^n\) as \(x^n\) only if at least half of these positions are flipped. In both cases, the farther apart the two codewords are, the more accidents the channel must produce, and the less likely the confusion. The same is true on every channel.

Proposition (two codewords). On a binary-input memoryless channel,

\[P(x^n\to\tilde x^n)\ \le\ \beta^{\,d_H(x^n,\tilde x^n)},\qquad\text{where}\quad \beta := \sum_{y}\sqrt{p(y\mid 0)\,p(y\mid 1)}.\]
Proof.

If \(b\ge a\ge 0\), then \(a\le\sqrt{ab}\). So

\[P(x^n\to\tilde x^n) = \sum_{y^n} p(y^n\mid x^n)\,\ind{p(y^n\mid\tilde x^n)\ge p(y^n\mid x^n)}\ \le\ \sum_{y^n}\sqrt{p(y^n\mid x^n)\,p(y^n\mid\tilde x^n)},\]

and the last sum is \(\prod_{i=1}^n\sum_{y}\sqrt{p(y\mid x_i)\,p(y\mid\tilde x_i)}\) by (1.1). A position with \(x_i=\tilde x_i\) gives the factor \(\sum_y p(y\mid x_i) = 1\), and a position where they differ gives \(\beta\).

The number \(\beta\) (an integral for continuous \(y\)) is the Bhattacharyya parameter of the channel. It is \(0\) for a noiseless channel and \(1\) for a useless one, where \(p(y\mid 0) = p(y\mid 1)\). On the BEC, \(\beta=\varepsilon\) and the bound is exact. On the BSC, \(\beta = 2\sqrt{\delta(1-\delta)}\), which is \(0.6\) for \(\delta=0.1\) and \(0.2\) for \(\delta = 0.01\). In the Gaussian example, \(\beta = e^{-E_s/N_0}\) (Lecture 8).

All codewords. If ML is wrong when \(x^n\) is sent, at least one other codeword is at least as likely as \(x^n\). The probability that one of several events occurs is at most the sum of their probabilities, so the error probability is at most the sum of the pairwise ones. The largest terms come from the nearest codewords. The minimum distance of a code \(\Ccal\) is the smallest distance between two of its codewords, \(d := \min_{x^n\neq\tilde x^n\in\Ccal} d_H(x^n,\tilde x^n)\).

Theorem (error probability). For a binary code with \(M\) codewords and minimum distance \(d\) on a binary-input memoryless channel, ML decoding has

\[P_e\ \le\ \max_{x^n\in\Ccal}\sum_{\tilde x^n\ne x^n} \beta^{\,d_H(x^n,\tilde x^n)}\ \le\ (M-1)\,\beta^{d}. \tag{1.8}\]

The minimum distance is the exponent: each extra unit of distance multiplies the bound by \(\beta\). The code of Example 1.5 has \(d=3\): every codeword has two neighbours at distance \(3\) and one at distance \(4\). So \(P_e\le 2\beta^3+\beta^4\), which is \(0.0021\) on the BEC\((0.1)\) and \(0.017\) on the BSC\((0.01)\). On a bad channel the bound is crude for such a short code (\(0.56\) on the BSC\((0.1)\)), but the exponent is right.

What is sure. The same counting tells when no pairwise error can occur at all.

Proposition (guarantees from \(d\)). If the channel makes at most \(d-1\) erasures (BEC), or at most \(\lfloor (d-1)/2\rfloor\) errors (BSC, \(\delta<\tfrac12\)), then no pairwise error event occurs: the sent codeword is strictly the most likely, and ML decoding is correct.

Proof.

Let \(\tilde x^n\) be any other codeword. As seen above, the event “\(\tilde x^n\) is at least as likely as \(x^n\)” needs all the \(d_H(x^n,\tilde x^n)\ge d\) differing positions to be erased on the BEC, and at least half of them, so at least \(d/2\), to be flipped on the BSC. Neither can happen with at most \(d-1\) erasures, or with at most \(\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor<d/2\) errors.

A code corrects about twice as many erasures as errors: knowing where the channel hit is worth a lot. With \(d=3\), the code of Example 1.5 is sure to correct one error or two erasures: its two wrong hard decisions were beyond the guarantee, its two erasures within it. It follows that \(P_e\) is at most the probability of more than \(\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor\) flips on the BSC, and of at least \(d\) erasures on the BEC (exercise 1.6).

On a general channel nothing is sure, because any number of LLRs can point the wrong way, but (1.8) still holds. On every channel, a good code needs distance. Lecture 2 asks what distance costs in rate.

To remember.

  • A message \(w\in\{1,\ldots,M\}\) is sent as the codeword \(x^n(w)\); \(R = (\log_2M)/n\), and \(P_e = P(\hat{\rv W}\ne\rv W)\) is the probability of a wrong message.
  • The best decoder is MAP, \(\hat w=\arg\max_w p(w)\,p(y^n\mid x^n(w))\). With a uniform message it is ML: \(\hat x^n=\arg\max_{x^n\in\Ccal} p(y^n\mid x^n)\). On a memoryless channel it needs, for each channel use, the likelihood function \(a\mapsto p(y_i\mid a)\) up to a factor.
  • Binary input: one number per bit, the LLR \(L=\ln\frac{p(y\mid0)}{p(y\mid1)}\). BSC: \(L=\pm\ln\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\). BEC: \(L\in\{+\infty,0,-\infty\}\). In general: any \(L\).
  • ML \(=\arg\min_{x^n\in\Ccal}\sum_{i:\,x_i\ne\hat y_i}|L_i|\), where \(\hat y_i\) is the hard decision: each disagreement costs its reliability. Hamming distance on the BSC, consistency on the BEC, weighted Hamming distance in general (equivalently, the nearest point \(\pm1\) to \(L^n\)).
  • Two codewords are confused with probability at most \(\beta^{d_H(x^n,\tilde x^n)}\), so \(P_e\le(M-1)\beta^d\): the minimum distance \(d\) is the exponent. For sure, \(d-1\) erasures (BEC) and \(\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor\) errors (BSC) are corrected.
